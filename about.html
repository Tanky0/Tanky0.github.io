About Me

My name is Anne Dayer, AI Governance Lead for the global Central Laboratory and Bioanalytical services of Labcorp, a US-based life sciences company with a global footprint. Based in Switzerland with over twenty years navigating the intersection of life sciences, data strategy, and innovation, current responsibilities encompass AI strategy, governance, and portfolio oversight. This includes identifying and selecting appropriate AI projects, establishing operating models, driving adoption of AI tools by staff and clients, and developing general AI literacy across the organisation. Pursuing an MSc in Artificial Intelligence at the University of Essex Online builds the technical depth needed to bridge strategic vision with responsible implementation.

Professional Skills Matrix and Action Plan

One current assessment of my professional skills across the programme, each skill carrying its own development plan in the final column. Revision happens as each module completes. The table began life as Appendix A of the Numerical Analysis End of Module Assessment 2 reflection, and the ratified marks and tutor feedback for that module have since reshaped several rows.

Self-assessment scale: Developing, Competent, Proficient, in ascending order. Benchmarks: Launch into Computing, 76 (October 2025); Understanding Artificial Intelligence, 87 (January 2026); Numerical Analysis, 81 (April 2026). Promotion to a higher level is claimed only where a module worked directly on the skill concerned. Elsewhere the gain sits inside the existing band, and what remains unclosed is work for the modules ahead and for the dissertation.

Module start and Module end refer to levels assessed across Numerical Analysis. Progress against each action is then tracked through Intelligent Agents, which began on 28 July 2026. Entries go in once a unit's assignments and activities are finished, not once marks return, so the column records work done rather than work graded.

Critical thinking & analysis Competent Competent (uneven) I reworked Brown's (1994) Table 2 and then had to defend the encoding choices. A peer raised a selection effect, which changed my reading. Normality was checked before tests were chosen, rather than reaching for the familiar ones. The ratified marks temper this: criticality reached 20/25 in the reflective work, credited for justifying choices, yet only 10/15 in the statistical presentation, where alternative perspectives went unpresented and the predictive model was left without hold-out or external validation. The gap sits between reflecting critically and analysing critically. LO2 is where that habit needs to travel: applying and critically evaluating intelligent agent techniques to real-world problems, particularly where technical risk and uncertainty is involved. The same scrutiny belongs with AI model validation evidence reviewed at work. Collaborative Discussion 1 tested this directly. My initial post placed the human checkpoint at plan decomposition, then two classmates showed that such a checkpoint cannot detect a retrieval stage lost after approval, so the summary post gave the position up. Reading Brooks (1991) against Bratman, Israel and Pollack (1988) explained why neither architecture keeps a record of what it discarded.

IT & digital Developing Developing (improved) Working R scripts across Units 8 to 12 (readr, dplyr, haven, ggplot2), written and debugged without any prior programming background, from a baseline of 76 in Launch into Computing. Numeracy Developing Competent 96% (Distinction) in the mid-module mathematics test. Chi-square, correlation (r = 0.847), regression with confidence intervals and Bayes' theorem were all computed and then checked in R. Regression and probability feed straight into Machine Learning, where linear regression returns under supervised learning and is followed by model evaluation and optimisation, so both deserve revisiting before Unit 1, and model performance statistics at work warrant the same scrutiny. Quantitative reading has already done real work. Wang et al.'s (2016) batch times, rising from 5,660 to 20,005 seconds under two agent failures, costed the resilience claim in my initial post. Zhan et al. (2026) then separated a rare failure mode from a common one, since error propagation runs at 0.002 to 0.017 whilst restriction neglect reaches 0.29.
Research Competent Competent (sourcing thin in analytical work) Reading went beyond the list to Schober, Bossers and Schwarte (2018), van de Schoot et al. (2021) and Kankaraš and Capecchi (2025) for the forum work and the reflection, where references were marked as used effectively (8/10). Sources in the statistical presentation scored 7/10. The framing carried citations; the analysis largely did not, with the age-group plateau offered as an example of a claim left unsupported. Thoroughness in going beyond the reading list is worth keeping, and it transfers to the agent and multi-agent literature as the module runs. One caveat carries forward from the presentation feedback. Sourcing has to reach the analytical work itself, not only the framing placed around it. Fourteen sources across the four Collaborative Discussion 1 pieces, with citations working inside the arguments rather than sitting around them. Reviewing F.B.'s Section B, I checked the cited sources rather than the argument and found two that did not support the claims resting on them: a review covering clinical trial matching rather than literature curation, and error rates taken from clinical documentation. A better-fitting reference replaced both of them.

Ethical awareness Proficient Proficient The Brown (1994) redesign showed how category and chart choices shape the conclusion a reader draws, which connects directly to the data integrity principles (GCP, ALCOA+) already governing my work.
