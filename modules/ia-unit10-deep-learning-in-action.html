My Post

Automated pain assessment in laboratory animals

Overview

GrimACE scores mouse pain cage-side, combining the Mouse Grimace Scale with pose estimation (Sturman et al., 2026). It rates five facial action units, 0 to 2, but manual scoring is laborious, limiting uptake (Andresen et al., 2020; Tuttle et al., 2018).

How it works

Networks train on cropped facial images labelled by scorers or treatment condition, learning their own features (Kobayashi et al., 2025). Architectures scaled from InceptionV3 on 6,862 images (Tuttle et al., 2018) to 545,000 (Kobayashi et al., 2025), then transformers (Sturman et al., 2026). These are perception components, not autonomous agents pursuing delegated objectives (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 21).

Impacts

Neither system learns pain directly: Tuttle et al. (2018) treat the human rating as truth, reporting 84% accuracy, reaching 94% only by discarding a third of images; Kobayashi et al. (2025) make treatment the proxy, labelling pre-injection images as no-pain. False negatives leave suffering undetected, yet such scores inform severity classification under Directive 2010/63/EU (2010), covering 3.56 million mice annually (European Commission, 2024). Benefits are real: it detected pain 48 hours post-craniotomy and found meloxicam alone sufficient (Sturman et al., 2026). Automation displaces the observer best placed to contest a score, and attributing responsibility is difficult (Ammanath and Firth-Butterfield, 2022). Refinement may substitute for replacement as the Commission targets phase-out for chemical safety assessments (European Commission, 2026).
