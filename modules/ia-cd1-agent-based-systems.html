The Activity

Units 1–3 · 28 July to 17 August 2026 · Tutor: Dr Angesh Anupam · Evidences learning outcomes 1 and 4

Discussion question: discuss what has led to the rise of agent-based systems and the benefits that this approach can offer to organisations, using at least three references.

Additional task: research how LLM-powered agents are designed for academic research or information gathering, summarise the key ideas, explain how they could support my own agent design, and describe one design decision I plan to make for the team project.

Structure set by the brief: an initial post of at least 200 words in Unit 1, at least two peer responses of 200–300 words in Unit 2, and a 300-word summary post in Unit 3 drawing on the initial post, the peer feedback and the content of all three units. UoEO Harvard referencing throughout.

Where the brief states a single figure rather than a range, I have worked to the University’s 10% tolerance on word counts, which is why the initial and summary posts sit slightly above 300 rather than exactly on it.

The forum contribution is not individually marked. It is a component of the e-portfolio submitted in Unit 12, which is where the assessment happens.

Two separate strands, not one sequence

The activity produced two things that are easy to confuse, so they are kept apart on this page.

My own thread runs initial post, then feedback from three classmates, then summary post. The summary post answers their feedback, and this is the only strand in which my position changes.

Responses I wrote on other students’ threads are three separate contributions to three different discussions. They are not replies to the people who replied to me, and nothing in them feeds into my summary post.

Six classmates are involved in total: three who commented on my post, and three whose posts I commented on. They are identified by initials throughout, including inside the posts as reproduced, because this e-portfolio is publicly accessible.

What was produced

One initial post (329 words), three responses on other students’ threads (291, 282 and 280 words) where two were required, and one summary post (328 words). Fourteen sources across the four pieces, of which only Wooldridge appears in more than two, and in a different passage each time.

The three responses deliberately run on separate evidence bases: measured failure rates, semantic interoperability, and the quality of industry evidence. Writing three rather than two was a choice, and the reasoning sits in the running reflection on the module page.

My Thread: Initial Post

Unit 1 · 28 July to 3 August 2026 · 329 words

Agent-based computing is traced to five trends: ubiquity, interconnection, intelligence, delegation and human orientation (Wooldridge, 2009). Cheap connectivity made distribution viable; rising task complexity made delegation attractive. A new abstraction became necessary once delegation crossed organisational boundaries, where no shared goal holds: one system cannot simply invoke another’s method, since the decision to execute sits with the invoker in object systems, and with the receiving agent in agent systems (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 29).

Three organisational benefits follow, beginning with flexibility. Wang et al. (2016) model machines, conveyors and products as agents bidding through the contract net protocol (Smith, 1980), so routes emerge from negotiation rather than central scheduling, making small-batch customisation feasible. The second is fault tolerance: their tinting simulation completed batches even when agents malfunctioned. The third is graceful degradation, which Brooks (1991) attributes to the absence of a central world model: environmental change disables particular layers, not the whole controller.

That same simulation shows the cost. With a machine agent down, cheaper deadlock-prevention strategies fail, and Wang et al.’s batch time rises from 5,660s to 9,490s, then 20,005s when a second agent fails. Resilience is bought here, not given.

For the additional task, Zhan et al. (2026) audit six deep research agents across the plan-search-summarise trajectory. Restriction neglect, the proportion of user constraints silently dropped during planning, ranges from 0.04 for OpenAI to 0.29 for Salesforce. Grok cannot be scored, since its interface hides planning traces. Our team is building a two-agent pipeline for clinical literature: a planning agent decomposes the query, a curation agent ranks what returns. I will therefore argue for approval at the decomposition, not the summary: a reviewer can confirm that “randomised trials only” survived planning, but cannot audit a landscape never searched. Adjustable autonomy returns control when uncertainty is high, or harm is possible (Scerri et al., 2003, cited in Wooldridge, 2009, p. 23).

Does a plan-stage checkpoint survive the reruns as evidence accumulates, when nobody is present to approve?

My Thread: Feedback Received

Unit 2 · 4 to 10 August 2026 · Three classmates replied to my initial post

Summarised in my own words rather than reproduced. The UoEO referencing guide asks students to check with their tutor before formally citing another student’s work, so each contribution below is credited by initials and by argument, with nothing republished.

A.B.: name the model the argument assumes

My post leaned on a belief-desire-intention account of planning without once naming it, and A.B. said so. Naming it would anchor the argument in the literature the module actually sets. Two further suggestions came with that: develop why human orientation matters among the five trends, and connect restriction neglect to work treating trust and control as complementary rather than opposed.

What I did with it. Naming the model exposed the flaw in my own position. What arrived as a presentational note became the hinge of the summary post.

A.N.: completion is a poor proxy for reliability

Completion, A.N. argued, can conceal silent degradation. A batch that finishes slowly is visibly impaired. A clinical research agent that finishes whilst missing relevant trials still looks successful, and the evidence offered for that ran across three strands: omission in language-model outputs, model confidence that persists when the answer is wrong, and oversight that depends first on people being able to detect an error at all. The conclusion followed. A plan-stage checkpoint cannot guarantee that retrieval, ranking and summarising preserve a constraint once an agent has failed, so resilience should be measured through output quality, with the system declaring failed agents, affected stages and evidence coverage.

What I did with it. This is the objection that moved me. The disclosure requirement in the summary post is that proposal carried straight into the design.

Y.A.F.: one approval cannot govern a system that keeps running

Y.A.F. accepted the checkpoint, then pressed on its scope. One approval point is not enough for agents that keep operating and keep responding to new information. The case drew on work about keeping capable systems compatible with human intentions, on combining automation with meaningful human control, and on risk-management guidance that treats governance as continuous monitoring rather than a single sign-off.

What I did with it. Oversight stopped being a moment and became a property of what the system emits over time. That is where the summary post ends.

My Thread: Summary Post

Unit 3 · 11 to 17 August 2026 · 328 words

My initial post traced agent-based computing to five converging trends and argued for approval at the decomposition stage, since a reviewer can confirm a constraint survived planning but cannot audit an unsearched landscape. A.N. and Y.A.F. both answered no: necessary, but not sufficient.

A.N. identified a failure my checkpoint could not detect. A batch finishing slowly is visibly impaired, whereas a clinical agent finishing without relevant trials still appears successful. Wang et al. (2016) could measure degradation, since batch time rose from 5,660 to 20,005 seconds, whereas omission carries no such measure.

Unit 3 sets two architectures side by side. Brooks (1991) keeps no central world model, leaving nothing to inspect. Bratman, Israel and Pollack (1988) do represent intentions, though an intention is a desire already adopted and structured into a plan, whose function is to constrain further practical reasoning. Filtered options are not routinely revisited. For opposite reasons, neither architecture retains a record of what it discarded, which would explain why Zhan et al. (2026), in a preprint, measure restriction neglect externally.

Unit 2 names that structure precisely. “Randomised trials only” is the premise of a universally quantified implication, and Russell and Norvig (2022, p. 279) warn that substituting conjunction for implication changes the sentence entirely. Restriction neglect is that premise quietly disappearing.

A.B. had asked me to name the model my argument presupposed, and naming it revealed the flaw: I justified the checkpoint because coverage is unauditable afterwards, then inspected only the plan.

Bratman’s filter override supplies the remedy, since such an override must have something to act upon. Each stage should declare failed agents, affected stages and unexecuted subquestions, which is what Y.A.F.’s case for continuous oversight requires. In a regulated laboratory, an auditor cannot review what was never made visible. Adjustable autonomy returns control when uncertainty is high (Scerri et al., 2003, cited in Wooldridge, 2009, p. 23), yet a pipeline blind to its own coverage loss never registers it.

Responses I Wrote on Other Students’ Threads

Unit 2 · 4 to 10 August 2026 · Three responses where two were required

These sit apart from the thread above. They were written on three other students’ initial posts, so the classmates answered here are not the ones who commented on mine, and nothing in them feeds into my summary post.

The brief asks each response to concentrate on the measures that could have prevented the issue raised. Each of the three identifies a different failure and proposes a different control, so none recycles the reasoning of another.

Response 1: on a coordinating planning agent

291 words · Peer post argued for a single coordinating planner drawing on STORM and PaSa

Hi L.K.,

Your Bouslimi paragraph is the part I keep returning to, since it names a cost most of us skipped: efficiency held as complexity rose, yet specialised roles and rigid protocols narrowed flexibility.

That is why your coordinating planning agent gives me pause. Concentrating decomposition, sequencing and control in one component compounds the rigidity Bouslimi (2024) reports, and restores the central world model whose absence Brooks (1991) links to robustness.

Where the risk sits surprised me, though. In a preprint, Zhan et al. (2026) measure error propagation across research trajectories at 0.002 to 0.017, so a flawed plan corrupting later stages is real but rare. Restriction neglect, where planning silently drops a stated user constraint, runs from 0.04 to 0.29. Their systems are general research agents, not specialist searchers, yet the failure looks architectural. The likelier planning failure is omission, not cascade.

That distinction bears on both your exemplars. STORM settles its perspectives during pre-writing, and PaSa’s Selector judges each paper in the queue against the user’s criteria (Shao et al., 2024; He et al., 2025). That queue holds only what the Crawler fetched. A constraint lost during query generation is unlikely to be recovered, since citation expansion follows the neighbourhood of what was already found. Evaluation after retrieval cannot catch an omission it never sees.

The measure I would add sits before retrieval, not after. Expose the generated subquestions for approval, so a reviewer confirms that a stated restriction survived decomposition. That is adjustable autonomy in the strict sense, returning control when uncertainty is high (Scerri et al., 2003, cited in Wooldridge, 2009, p. 23).

Would you let the retrieval agent challenge the plan when evidence does not fit, or keep every revision with the planner?

Anne

Response 2: on the objects-to-agents shift

282 words · Peer post argued the move from passive objects to goal-pursuing agents was the enabling leap

Hi L.M.F.,

Your objects-to-agents framing is the strongest part of the post, and Wooldridge sharpens it further than you claim. Section 2.2 concedes that programmers used to Java see little novelty in agents, and later grants that agents can still be built with object-oriented techniques (Wooldridge, 2009, pp. 28–29). What the basic object model lacks is not capability but control over behaviour, since once a method is public the invoker decides. The leap is about who keeps the right to refuse.

That bites hardest exactly where you place your examples, because the same passage notes the problem matters most for agents built by different organisations.

Here is the difficulty I would add. If the receiver decides, it decides on its own reading of the request. Finin et al. (1994) saw this while proposing KQML: standardised performatives fix the message, yet interpreting it depends on knowledge the message does not carry, and the authors expected many ontologies rather than one. Payne and Tamma (2014) quantify what remains. Agents holding private, incomplete and asymmetric correspondences have to negotiate an alignment, and their negotiated result reaches around 95% of what central alignment with complete information achieves.

Which is why I would settle the mapping before anything is committed. In lab operations, sample received and sample accessioned are routinely different events, and two systems will reconcile straight past that gap without either flagging it. Payne and Tamma’s agents disclose part of their private correspondence set before agreeing anything; lacking an equivalent step, smooth coordination only shows that the messages parsed.

Your stakeholders sometimes compete, though, as you note. Would a supplier’s agent volunteer its own vocabulary when the mismatch happens to favour it?

Anne

Response 3: on the industry evidence base

280 words · Peer post built the organisational case on consultancy and analyst sources

Hi F.O.,

Including Gartner as a counterweight is the strongest move here. Most of us listed benefits and stopped, whereas you closed on the condition under which they fail to arrive.

I went back to the PwC survey, though, and I think that figure reads differently. It reports 66% of 300 senior executives seeing productivity rise, which is the share answering yes rather than the size of any gain (PwC, 2025); a 66% increase in work productivity would be an extraordinary result, and I do not think the survey claims one.

If that is right, it strengthens your case. Read that way, PwC and Gartner stop contradicting each other. Executives who authorised the spending perceive improvement, while Gartner forecasts over 40% of projects cancelled for escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls (Gartner, 2025). A perception survey cannot measure output, only what respondents believe about output. What sits between the two is the redesign you describe, since automating a task changes a workflow whereas realising value means moving responsibility.

Your sources answer different questions as well. Wooldridge (2009) settles what an agent is, while the industry material reports how adoption is currently perceived, and perception is not the same evidence as outcome.

The measure I would settle before deployment is therefore what counts as realised benefit, certified by someone other than the sponsor. Bratman, Israel and Pollack (1988) argue that agents commit to intentions because deliberation is costly under bounded resources, and supervision shifts that boundedness onto human attention rather than dissolving it.

If oversight is what keeps a project off Gartner’s cancellation list, does the supervisory load undermine the headcount economics that motivated it?

Anne
