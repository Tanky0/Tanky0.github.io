Module Overview

Intelligent Agents examines the design and implementation of autonomous agents: systems that perceive an environment, reason about it, and act within it to pursue objectives. The module covers agent architectures and rational decision-making, agent communication languages and dialogue, parsing and knowledge exchange between agents, multi-agent coordination, and deep learning applied to agent behaviour.

Core reading is drawn from Russell and Norvig's Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach and Wooldridge's An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems. The module builds directly on the AI foundations established in Understanding Artificial Intelligence and on the probabilistic reasoning developed in Numerical Analysis. It is the first module of the programme assessed on work produced as part of a development team.

Module Aims and Learning Outcomes

This module aims to instil amongst students:

  • An understanding of the motivations for, and appropriate use of, agent-based computing.
  • An understanding of the main agent models in use today and their grounding in artificial intelligence research.
  • The knowledge and skills required to develop, deploy and evaluate the tools and techniques of intelligent systems to solve real-world problems.
  • An understanding of contemporary research issues in the area of intelligent agent systems.

On completion of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Identify and critically analyse agent-based systems, differentiating between architectures and approaches.
  2. Apply and critically evaluate intelligent agent techniques to real-world problems, particularly where technical risk and uncertainty is involved.
  3. Deploy critically appropriate software tools and skills for the design and implementation of an agent-based system, bearing in mind applicable legal, social, ethical and professional issues.
  4. Systematically develop and implement the skills required to be an effective member of a development team in a virtual professional environment, adopting real-life perspectives on team roles and organisation.

Units 1 and 2: what the exchanges gave back

Written 7 August 2026 · Collaborative Discussion 1, opening posts · Team formation

What. Two strands ran in parallel. In the collaborative discussion I posted on the rise of agent-based systems, then wrote three peer responses where two were required. On the team project I posted the opening proposal, the contract was signed on 31 July, and the kick-off that day settled roles, with Group Coordinator falling to me.

So what. The peer exchanges turned out to be the most productive part of the fortnight. Writing three responses instead of two was deliberate. Each classmate had framed the question differently, and working through someone else’s framing reaches things my own post never would.

One exchange stands out. A classmate had set a PwC adoption survey against a Gartner cancellation forecast. Going back to the survey showed the figure reported the share of executives answering yes, not the size of any gain, so the two sources were not in conflict after all. I hesitated before posting that. Correcting someone publicly in the second week of a module is a quick way to be read as competitive, and I record the hesitation because it nearly cost the exchange. Framing settled it: offered as a reading of the source rather than a correction of its author, the point strengthened the argument and was taken that way. Checking each other’s numbers is routine where I work, and it does the same job here, which is to make what the group produces more reliable than what any of us produces alone.

The Zhan et al. (2026) figures moved my own position, and that is the more valuable outcome. I had assumed the risk in a planning agent sat in cascade, a poor plan corrupting everything downstream of it. Error propagation runs at 0.002 to 0.017, whilst restriction neglect, where a stated constraint is silently dropped during decomposition, runs from 0.04 to 0.29. Being wrong by an order of magnitude about where the risk sits was uncomfortable in a productive way. It is now the reason I argue for oversight at decomposition in our own design rather than at the output.

The team side has been straightforwardly generative. The use case we adopted came from a teammate’s proposal rather than either of the two I had put forward, and it is the better idea. Saying early what I am strongest at, and what I would rather leave to someone else, is something I do routinely as a manager, and it did here what it usually does, which is move work to whoever is best placed for it. Flagging the unverified parts of my own input, a Crossref capability I had not checked and a position I had revised between drafts, is ordinary practice rather than an admission.

Now what. Three things carry forward. Keep flagging what is unverified, since it costs a sentence and protects the argument the report rests on. Keep checking figures against their source before building on them, in my own drafts as much as in anyone else’s. And in the challenges section, hold the position the evidence supports, because naming what we cannot build on a free tier is more defensible than claiming coverage we cannot deliver.

Unit 3: giving up a position I had argued for

Written 11 August 2026 · Collaborative Discussion 1, summary post

What. My initial post argued that restriction neglect happens during planning, so the control belongs at the plan stage: a reviewer can confirm a stated constraint survived decomposition, whereas nobody can audit a landscape that was never searched. Three classmates replied. By the summary post the position had changed, and the Unit 3 reading is what changed it.

So what. Two of the replies reached the same structural point from different directions. The checkpoint inspects the plan and nothing else, so a pipeline that loses a retrieval stage after approval returns a polished summary and announces nothing. The control cannot see the failure it was installed to prevent. What made that awkward is that the flaw was already sitting inside my own argument. I had justified the checkpoint on the grounds that coverage is unauditable after the fact, then built a mechanism that looks only at the plan. Reading it back was uncomfortable, and usefully so, because it stopped me defending the position and sent me looking for why neither architecture leaves a trace.

Unit 3 supplied the reason. Brooks (1991) keeps no central world model, so there is nothing to interrogate. Bratman, Israel and Pollack (1988) do represent intentions, but an intention is a desire already adopted into a plan whose function is to constrain further practical reasoning, and filtered options are not routinely revisited. Two architectures, opposite reasons, one consequence: neither retains a record of what it discarded. That is a plausible explanation for why Zhan et al. (2026) have to measure restriction neglect from outside the system.

A.B.’s reply is what made any of this visible. The request was that I name the model my argument presupposed, which I first read as a presentational note and nearly answered as one. Naming it exposed the flaw. I have stopped treating “state your assumptions” as a tidying step at the end of a draft, since here it worked as a test the argument failed.

Now what. Keep the checkpoint and pair it with disclosure. Bratman’s filter override is the mechanism that reopens a committed plan, and an override needs something to act upon: each stage should declare which agents failed, which stages were affected, and which approved subquestions went unexecuted. Carried into the team project, that means the planning agent surfaces its decomposition for approval and the curation agent reports coverage loss. Whether both are affordable inside the free-tier constraints we are working to is open, and leaving it open is more defensible than claiming it.

Units 1 to 3: the module itself, at the theory stage

Written 11 August 2026 · Stocktake on the module rather than on one artefact

What. Three units complete. Unit 1 set out the trends behind agent-based computing and Wooldridge’s distinction between an object that must execute and an agent that may decline. Unit 2 covered first-order logic. Unit 3 compared architectures, from Brooks’s subsumption through belief-desire-intention. Alongside them the first collaborative discussion has run its course and the team project is at draft stage. Nothing has been implemented yet.

So what. I expected this module to be the most useful of the programme, given where my industry is going, and it has been. Not in the way I expected, though. Much of the multi-agent material describes things I already deal with. Parties who did not build each other's systems cannot be assumed to want the same thing, and exchanges across organisational boundaries fail on meaning far more often than on transport. I had been handling both case by case for years without a name for either. Having the vocabulary has been worth more to me than learning something entirely new, because you cannot argue about a problem you cannot name.

The new material is technical, and that is where my gap is. I am not an engineer and I did not come from coding, so the notation in Unit 2 and the architecture in Unit 3 took real effort rather than recognition. That feels like the right way round. What I already know lets me judge whether the technical apparatus is being used sensibly. The apparatus itself is what I do not have.

Three units of theory and nothing built yet. I am impatient, and it has changed how I work. What I have done instead is treat every written artefact as though it were a specification for something real, asking whether the design would hold rather than whether the paragraph reads well.

Now what. Two things. Treat the implementation units as the test of whether any of this is real, since a design I cannot build is a design I have not understood. And point the judgement I bring from work at the technical material rather than keeping it separate. The risk for someone in my position is not failing to learn the tooling. It is carrying on as though governance and implementation were two conversations.

Unit 4: editing what the group wrote

Written 21 August 2026 · Development Team Project, v12 to v14

What. On 12 August I combined three separately drafted sections into one report and have carried the document since. Three versions followed. v12 cut the merged draft from 1,314 words to 1,088 and rewrote the success criteria; v13 restructured the design section and added a technology comparison; v14, written after the Unit 4 seminar on agent architectures, argues each design choice against the alternative we rejected.

So what. Combining the sections is the point at which the report stopped being three documents. What I had not expected is how much of the first pass was arithmetic. The draft would have crossed the penalty threshold before a marker read a sentence, and the single largest saving came from the architecture being restated in four separate sections, which no individual author could see because each had written only one of them. That is a structural property of dividing work by section, not a failing of anyone who wrote one.

Two findings were worth more than the tidying. The report claimed a free-tier toolchain and then leaned on a paid fallback two paragraphs later, so the claim was simply untrue as written. The success criteria were the more interesting problem. Retrieval quality was to be confirmed by the reviewer, which makes the acceptance test the same person the system exists to serve, so it passes whenever that person is satisfied. Writing it as recall of at least 0.80 against a hand-screened sample took an afternoon and turned an aspiration into something that can fail. I have written acceptance criteria professionally for years and still did not catch that reading until I was editing rather than drafting. It was invisible from the seat I wrote it in.

What made the editing workable was a realignment we made deliberately. Agreement on scope and structure came early, so the others could turn to their own components and to the module activities running in parallel while the writing adjustments stayed with me. Dividing work by strength rather than by equal shares is how I would run it professionally, and it is more efficient than four people editing one document. It also concentrates judgement in one place, which is the reason the change log matters: every edit carries its reason, so any of it can be reopened rather than simply inherited.

Now what. Three things. Ask about one decision at a time rather than sending the whole document round, because a specific question gets answered and an open invitation does not when everyone is busy with their own part. Keep writing criteria that can actually fail. A criterion nobody can fail is a sentence, not a control, and I write these at work as well. And read the design section again after the 1 September seminar before we lock it, since v14 only improved because material arrived after v13 was already finished.

Unit 6: what the dialogue exposed

Written 11 August 2026 · Creating Agent Dialogues (KQML / KIF)

What. Unit 6 asked for a KQML and KIF dialogue between Alice, who procures stock, and Bob, who controls stock levels for a warehouse. My first attempt ran to four messages and met the brief. The artefact I kept runs to nine, preceded by the ontology the exchange depends on, and adds two failure branches: a sorry where Alice asks for a term the shared vocabulary does not define, and an achieve that has no matching commitment.

So what. Four messages proved nothing beyond an ability to copy a syntax, which is not what a portfolio is for. The exercise only became worth doing once I asked what would have to be true before Alice and Bob could understand one another at all, and saw how little of that ever reaches the messages. Take :ontology warehouse-stock. It reads as a guarantee and it is a claim, unverifiable, since neither agent can inspect the other’s vocabulary. That single line made me more uneasy than anything in the dialogue itself, and the unease is why the ontology took longer to write than the nine messages did. Two commitments buried inside it, a marketing screen size rather than a measured one and a port count blind to revision, are the kind of thing I have watched surface late, once an integration has already produced numbers nobody trusts.

The parallel with my own work is direct. Central laboratory operations exchange data with sponsor systems daily, across organisational boundaries, and what breaks is hardly ever the pipe. It is two parties using one term for two things, undetected until a reconciliation refuses to balance. Recognising a familiar failure in unfamiliar dress slowed me down. That was the right response, since it stopped me treating the exercise as a syntax problem and sent me back to the definitions. Payne and Tamma (2014) frame the constraint as disclosure, since agents may have to align vocabularies neither side will publish. I had filed ontology alignment under schema matching, and read properly it looks at least as much like a negotiation problem, though I gave their remedy more enthusiasm than it has earned: their dialogue was tested on benchmark ontologies rather than in anyone’s warehouse.

The commissive gap is where the exercise changed my thinking. Bob’s tell is true at the moment of sending and worthless as an assurance, and my first move was to hunt for the performative I had overlooked. There is none. Being wrong about that was more useful than being right would have been. It stopped me reading a specification as complete and made me look for what it declines to do, which is now how I read any protocol I am asked to rely on. Wooldridge (2009) arrives at the same criticism, and it is why FIPA exists rather than a patched KQML. Writing the dialogue rather than coding it is also the concession I owe the module’s sequencing, since I would almost certainly have implemented straight past the missing commitment.

Now what. Three things carry forward. Read a specification for its silences before its vocabulary, because the missing act taught me more than the nine present ones. Treat a declared shared vocabulary as an assertion to be tested rather than a fact, here and at work. And implement M2 to M9 as a plain message-passing exercise in Python before Unit 12, where a lost eos or an unhandled sorry fails visibly instead of becoming another paragraph in a reflection. My confidence that the KIF is well formed currently rests on resemblance to published examples rather than on having run it, and I would not accept that standard of assurance from a supplier.

Unit 8: when the tree ran out

Written 18 August 2026 · Creating Parse Trees

What. Unit 8 asked for constituency parse trees of three sentences. I built each from a small phrase-structure grammar and drew it out, then let the third, The man saw the dog with the telescope, carry the work. One early step was noticing that the set reading demonstrates dependency parsing in spaCy, whereas the brief wanted constituency trees, since the two describe the same grammar from different angles (Zimmerman, 2019). My first pass treated the third sentence as an even ambiguity and drew both attachments. The version I kept does not: the instrument reading is the one the sentence has, and the other structure is left as a note on what the grammar will build but the meaning refuses.

So what. Drawing both trees and then throwing one away is what taught me the point. A parse can be perfectly well formed and still be the wrong reading, since a dog does not carry a telescope and no amount of syntax knows that; ranking the two structures is something a parser has to learn from data rather than from the grammar (Collins, 2003). The second sentence made the same case from the other side, because the internet gives everyone a voice has one tidy tree whether the giving is literal or a metaphor, so the structure records how the words combine and nothing about what they mean. What caught me off guard was how little the diagram holds once you look for meaning in it, which I had not expected from an exercise I had filed as mechanical.

The exercise was new in a plainer sense too. I had not drawn a syntax tree before, and I am neither an engineer nor a native English speaker, so I expected the notation to be the obstacle. It was not. The friction sat in the few places where English builds a phrase differently from French, and the core proved to be shared ground, since French rests on the same building blocks and even the telescope ambiguity survives translation. The vocabulary looked harder at the outset than the analysis turned out to be.

Now what. Two things. When a formalism is unfamiliar, treat the labels as the first obstacle and the reasoning as the second, because here the reasoning was nearer than the notation made it look. And carry the distinction into the deep-learning units: well formed is not the same as correct. A model producing fluent output is making exactly that claim about itself, and somebody has to check it.

Unit 11: building the thing I had only ever specified

Written 29 August 2026 · Development Project, individual implementation · In progress

What. Development started on 26 August, the morning after the tutor confirmed that evidence of progress has to build up across the module rather than appear at the end. I had time off from work and used it on this, close to full working days for four days running. That was what the unfamiliarity demanded rather than what the schedule did. The repository now holds a requirements table written before any code, the shared HTTP layer, four source adapters, the record formats both agents exchange, a hundred passing tests, and a hundred abstracts screened by hand. The two agents themselves are not built. I am writing this now because the finished version will not remember any of it.

So what. I have been next to people who build things for a long time: fourteen years administering a new-technologies consultancy and developing the business for a 3D printing workshop, twenty-five in clinical operations and project management, and AI governance more recently. Between them I learnt what a planning agent is for, what a retrieval layer does, and why state has to sit somewhere you can inspect it. None of it told me what to type into an empty file, or in what order. Theory gave me the shape and nothing about sequence, and sequence turned out to be most of the difficulty.

The Python has been the hardest work of the programme so far, R included. It is not the syntax. It is that every decision I would once have handed to somebody else now has to be made by me, written down, and either runs or does not. Four days at that pace was not enthusiasm. It was the only way to hold enough of the thing in my head at once to get anywhere with it.

Three things I did not expect. The first was diagnostic rather than technical. All three services failed identically on the first day, and identical failure across three independent things points at one local cause rather than three faults; it was the antivirus intercepting HTTPS. That reasoning is the part of my professional judgement that transferred intact, and it is the only part that did.

The second was that measurement can be broken before anything is measured. I drew fifty abstracts at random and screened them by hand, and three came back relevant. With three, each is worth a third of the recall figure, so the only score clearing our 0.80 criterion is a perfect one, and missing a single paper fails it outright. That is a property of the sample rather than of the search. A second fifty was drawn as a check, and the design's figure of fifty stays as the reported basis rather than being quietly replaced by a number that flatters the result.

The third is where I have been most wrong, and it concerns my own carelessness rather than the code. Invented replies reached the folder the demonstration plays back from, twice, and on neither occasion was it a check that caught them. The guard test looked for the identifiers this project's own tests use, so it recognised contamination whose shape it already knew, and the second incident used different identifiers and passed straight through. Capture and publication are now two separate things, and each approved reply carries a fingerprint in a manifest, so a file nobody approved is refused whatever it contains. A check that only detects the failure you have already seen is not a control, and I would not have accepted one at work.

One decision sits outside the code. The repository's early history was reconstructed, and the reconstruction is a tidier record of the same files rather than a record of when the work happened. Rather than let the commit dates imply a diligence they cannot evidence, the development log says so plainly, the original history is kept on a separate branch, and the dated files under evidence are named as the real record. The assessment asks for evidence of development progress, and evidence that overstates itself is worth less than evidence that declares its own limits.

The Unit 11 reading sits at a distance from what I am building. Wang et al. (2016) describe machines and products negotiating their own routes with no central scheduler, and the other three items on the list work the same way, from the bottom up, with behaviour emerging from many agents interacting. Mine has two agents, a fixed order between them, and a person who approves before anything is accepted. Reading four arguments for emergence whilst deliberately building something that does not emerge forced me to say why. A screening aid for systematic reviews has to be auditable, and behaviour nobody specified is behaviour nobody can review.

Now what. Three things carry forward. Write the two agents against the record formats already fixed, since the formats were written first precisely so that everything after them takes its shape from something settled. Keep the standard set out in my development plan, which is to explain each line unaided rather than assemble working code from assisted fragments, because the parts of this build I could not currently defend line by line are the parts I would not sign off from a supplier. And keep reporting the limitations beside the numbers: two of the relevant papers were judged on information the agent will never see, and that belongs next to the recall figure rather than in a footnote after it.
