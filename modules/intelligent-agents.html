Module Overview

Intelligent Agents examines the design and implementation of autonomous agents: systems that perceive an environment, reason about it, and act within it to pursue objectives. The module covers agent architectures and rational decision-making, agent communication languages and dialogue, parsing and knowledge exchange between agents, multi-agent coordination, and deep learning applied to agent behaviour.

Core reading is drawn from Russell and Norvig's Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach and Wooldridge's An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems. The module builds directly on the AI foundations established in Understanding Artificial Intelligence and on the probabilistic reasoning developed in Numerical Analysis. It is the first module of the programme assessed on work produced as part of a development team.

Module Aims and Learning Outcomes

This module aims to instil amongst students:

  • An understanding of the motivations for, and appropriate use of, agent-based computing.
  • An understanding of the main agent models in use today and their grounding in artificial intelligence research.
  • The knowledge and skills required to develop, deploy and evaluate the tools and techniques of intelligent systems to solve real-world problems.
  • An understanding of contemporary research issues in the area of intelligent agent systems.

On completion of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Identify and critically analyse agent-based systems, differentiating between architectures and approaches.
  2. Apply and critically evaluate intelligent agent techniques to real-world problems, particularly where technical risk and uncertainty is involved.
  3. Deploy critically appropriate software tools and skills for the design and implementation of an agent-based system, bearing in mind applicable legal, social, ethical and professional issues.
  4. Systematically develop and implement the skills required to be an effective member of a development team in a virtual professional environment, adopting real-life perspectives on team roles and organisation.

Creating Agent Dialogues

Unit 6 · Due end of Unit 6 · Evidences learning outcomes 1 and 2

Activity: Create an agent dialogue, using KQML and KIF, between two agents. Alice procures stock; Bob controls stock levels for a warehouse. Alice asks Bob about available stock of 50 inch televisions and about the number of HDMI slots the televisions have.

The artefact runs to nine KQML messages carrying KIF content, preceded by the warehouse-stock ontology the exchange depends on. It uses advertise, ask-if, tell, ask-all, reply, stream-all and eos, then adds two failure branches: a sorry where Alice asks for a term the shared ontology does not define, and an achieve that exposes KQML's missing commissives. Each message is mapped to its Searle category, and the language is assessed against the criticisms Wooldridge sets out and against Finin and colleagues' own admission that the performative set was never closed.

Running reflections

Kept as the module proceeds, structured on Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001): What / So what / Now what.

Units 1 and 2: what the exchanges gave back

Written 7 August 2026

What. Two strands ran together. In the collaborative discussion I posted on the rise of agent-based systems, then wrote three peer responses rather than the two required. In the team project I posted the opening proposal, the contract was signed on 31 July, and the kick-off meeting that day settled roles, with Group Coordinator coming to me.

So what. The peer exchanges were the most productive part of the fortnight, which is the argument for treating them as work rather than as a box to tick. Writing three responses instead of two was deliberate. Each classmate had framed the question differently, and working through someone else’s framing surfaces things my own post never would have reached.

One exchange stands out. A classmate paired a PwC adoption survey against a Gartner cancellation forecast. Returning to the survey showed the figure reported the share of executives answering yes rather than the size of any gain, which meant his two sources were not in conflict after all. Putting that to him strengthened his argument rather than weakening it. Checking each other’s numbers is routine where I work, and it does the same job here: it makes what the group produces more reliable than what any of us would produce alone.

The Zhan et al. (2026) figures moved my own position, which is the more valuable outcome. I had assumed the risk in a planning agent sat in cascade, a poor plan corrupting everything after it. Error propagation runs at 0.002 to 0.017, while restriction neglect, where a stated constraint is silently dropped during decomposition, runs from 0.04 to 0.29. Evidence shifted my view by an order of magnitude, and it now sets where I argue oversight belongs in our own design.

The team side has been straightforwardly generative. The use case we adopted came from a teammate’s proposal rather than either of the two I had put forward, and it is the better idea. Saying early what I am strongest at and what I would rather leave to others is something I do routinely as a manager, and it did here what it usually does, which is move the work to whoever is best placed for it. Flagging the unverified parts of my own input, a Crossref capability I had not checked and a position I had revised between drafts, is ordinary practice rather than an admission.

Now what. Three things carry forward. Keep flagging what is unverified, since it costs a sentence and protects the argument the report rests on. Keep checking figures against their source before building on them, in my own drafts as much as in anyone else’s. And in the challenges section, hold the position the evidence supports, because naming what we cannot build on a free tier is more defensible than claiming coverage we cannot deliver.
