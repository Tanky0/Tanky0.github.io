Module Overview

Understanding Artificial Intelligence examines the essential concepts, principles, methods and problems of AI, together with the legal, ethical and professional issues these technologies raise and their wider impact on society. The module runs from foundational AI concepts and agent architectures, through machine learning paradigms and the CRISP-DM/CRISP-ML(Q) process models, to the practical application of modelling tools on a real dataset.

The four assessments moved deliberately from discussion to delivery: two collaborative learning discussions establishing conceptual and critical foundations, an individual consultancy-style essay proposing three AI use cases for a business, and a hands-on implementation report producing and evaluating actual models. For me the module was the point at which AI stopped being something I governed in the abstract and became something I had built, however modestly, and could therefore interrogate on its own terms.

Collaborative Learning Discussion 2: Machine Learning Algorithms and Their Contexts of Use

Brief Description: Three-week asynchronous discussion (Units 6–8) identifying and discussing two machine learning algorithms and the contexts in which they can be employed, considering supervised and unsupervised approaches and the strengths and weaknesses of each. Required an initial post of at least 200 words, responses to at least three peers, and a 300-word summary post.

Contribution: The discussion compared a supervised approach (Random Forest) against an unsupervised one (k-means) and their differing normative assumptions. Peer exchange reoriented the argument: one peer established that unsupervised methods carry their own assumptions through parameter selection, so neither paradigm is epistemically neutral; another identified that CRISP-DM's linearisation removes critical review at the deployment stage; a third pushed back hardest, arguing that bias is a data collection problem that no architectural choice can resolve. The summary post accepted that challenge and developed it through Celi et al. (2022) on dataset provenance, Jones et al. (2023) on the causal roots of data disparity, and Schröer et al. Individual Essay: Artificial Intelligence and its Applications

Brief Description: A 1,500-word consultancy report (40% of the module) persuading the senior management of a company with reservations about AI to adopt it. The report had to identify three key areas where AI could improve operations and return on investment, the problems likely to be encountered in each, the data required, the development approaches needed, and the strengths and weaknesses of each solution with reference to real deployments.

Submission: An AI transformation proposal for Labcorp Central Laboratory Services, framed against the Industry 4.0 to 5.0 transition. Three use cases were proposed: Protocol Intelligence, a retrieval-augmented advisory system for study design targeting the 76% protocol amendment rate at $141,000–$535,000 per event; Start-Up Kit Supply Optimisation, using gradient boosting with a quantile regression objective against ~$2.5m of excess kit per trial; and Automated Gating for flow cytometry using vendor-qualified platforms. Rather than adopt an off-the-shelf process model, the report compared CRISP-DM, CRISP-ML(Q) and the NIST AI RMF, showed that none alone covers the combined operational, regulatory and accountability demands of a GCP-regulated laboratory, and proposed a synthesised LC-CLS AI Framework with a non-removable human-in-the-loop layer, governance controls mapped to EU AI Act Article 9 and 21 CFR Part 11, before/after tables for each use case, and measurable IT KPIs.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) Solution Implementation

Brief Description: A report evidencing the implementation of an AI solution using an appropriate tool and dataset, with screenshots of model outputs and commentary demonstrating data pre- and post-processing, experiment design, training principles, algorithm selection and performance analysis in a justified business context.

Submission: A quantity forecasting feasibility study taking forward the kit supply use case proposed in the individual essay. Because LC-CLS production data engages GDPR Article 9 special category obligations, the experiment used the open USAID Supply Chain Management System delivery history (10,306 instances after cleaning, 43 countries) as a healthcare-domain proxy. Preprocessing reduced 33 columns to 12 analytical attributes, with two leaky features removed on the grounds that Line Item Value is derivable from the target by division. Following CRISP-ML(Q) quality gates, four regressors were evaluated under stratified 10-fold cross-validation in WEKA: ZeroR as baseline, Linear Regression for full coefficient traceability, M5P for auditable node-level rules, and Random Forest as the performance benchmark. Random Forest achieved R = 0.660 and MAE 13,011, a 43.0% reduction in mean absolute error over the baseline, confirmed by an 80/20 holdout. The report's central argument was that procedural validity and production readiness are different problems: the process, feature engineering and evaluation framework transfer to LC-CLS, but the performance figures do not, and a proxy-to-production mapping table set out exactly which elements fail to carry across.

Reflective Piece

What? (Description)

Understanding Artificial Intelligence ran across twelve units and four assessments. Two collaborative learning discussions came first: one on the case for AI investment in a chosen sector, one comparing supervised and unsupervised machine learning algorithms and their contexts of use. Both required an initial post, sustained peer engagement and a summary post evidencing how my position had moved. The individual essay then asked for a 1,500-word consultancy report proposing three AI use cases to a sceptical senior management team, which I wrote for Labcorp Central Laboratory Services, the organisation I actually work for. The final assessment required me to implement an AI solution, evaluate it, and report on the outputs; I built a regression feasibility study in WEKA on an open USAID healthcare supply chain dataset, running ZeroR, Linear Regression, M5P and Random Forest under 10-fold cross-validation.

So What? (Interpretation)

The most useful thing this module did was force the sequence: argue, then propose, then build. I came in as an AI governance lead, comfortable discussing frameworks, risk and accountability, and much less comfortable with the technical substrate underneath them. Writing the LC-CLS proposal was familiar territory; the implementation report was not, and it was the more valuable of the two.

The second collaborative discussion produced the sharpest correction. I had framed the choice between a supervised and an unsupervised approach as a question with a defensible technical answer. A peer argued that both operate on data whose representativeness was already determined before any practitioner made a paradigm choice, so bias is a collection problem that architecture cannot fix. Following that challenge into the literature – Celi et al. (2022) on the concentration of clinical AI datasets in two countries, Jones et al. (2023) on why bias mitigation applied at the model level routinely fails – changed what I think an AI audit can actually establish. Audit protocols detect deviation from intended behaviour; they cannot detect exact conformity to a misspecified intention. That distinction has since shaped how I frame governance review questions at work.

The first discussion produced a similar shift in a different direction. My initial post treated governance as the mechanism that converts technical capability into operational value, effectively as a precondition to be satisfied before deployment. Peers pointed out that trust develops alongside governance rather than from it, and that well-resourced systems still fail when governance fragments across jurisdictions. I had conflated regulatory compliance with operational readiness. The revised position – governance, trust, data stewardship and workforce readiness as interdependent conditions requiring co-calibration – is one I now use directly in my professional role.

The implementation report was where the module's assumptions met my own. Having proposed gradient boosting with a quantile regression objective in the essay, I had to establish whether the underlying claim held at all, and the honest answer was partial. Random Forest reduced mean absolute error by 43% over the naive baseline, which confirms a real signal. But the errors concentrated in the high-volume tail and in very small shipments, the proxy dataset lacked the site-level features that matter most in the real problem, and the operational asymmetry – a site short of kits delays patient visits, whereas surplus stock only wastes cold-chain logistics – is not something a symmetric loss function represents. Writing that down as a limitation rather than smoothing over it was the point at which the work felt genuinely like analysis rather than advocacy.

What Next? (Outcome)

Two pieces of tutor feedback pointed the same way. The second discussion suggested that evaluation techniques and scenario analysis would strengthen the argument for choosing one learning algorithm over another; the implementation report noted that the analysis relied too heavily on a single performance measure. Both identify the same gap: I could select and run models, but I could not yet interrogate their performance with the range or rigour the question deserved. That gap set the agenda for Numerical Analysis, where the work on p-values, confidence intervals and diagnostic plots gave me the vocabulary I was missing here, and it remains the through-line into Machine Learning.

Concretely, the outcomes I set at the end of this module were: to stop treating a single headline metric as an evaluation; to learn enough statistics to challenge a model performance claim rather than accept it; to move from tool-driven work in a GUI towards programmatic modelling; and to carry the proxy-to-production discipline – being explicit about which parts of an experiment transfer and which do not – into every subsequent piece of technical work. Professionally, the LC-CLS proposal is not a hypothetical exercise: the governance framework, the human-in-the-loop layer and the KPI structure developed for it are directly applicable to the AI portfolio I oversee, and the module's central lesson – that problem specification is an open negotiation rather than a technical precondition – is the question I now ask first in any AI review.
