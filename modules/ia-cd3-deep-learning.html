Collaborative Discussion 3: Deep Learning

Whether generative deep learning raises ethical issues worth considering, argued from what a provenance mark can and cannot settle, with two peer responses on bias and on training data.

The Activity

Units 9–11 · Tutor: Dr Angesh Anupam · Evidences learning outcome 3

Discussion question: new technologies supported by deep learning models make it possible to generate new content, for example DALL-E to generate images or ChatGPT to create prose. Do you think that these new technologies offer any ethical issues that should be considered, and if not, why not?

Structure set by the brief: an initial post of at least 200 words in Unit 9, responses to at least two peers’ posts in Unit 10 of 200 to 300 words each, focusing on the measures that could have prevented the issues those peers raise, and a 300-word summary post in Unit 11 drawing on the initial post, the peer feedback and the content of all three units. UoEO Harvard referencing throughout.

The forum contribution is not individually assessed; the tutor posts group feedback by module announcement. It is a component of the e-portfolio submitted in Unit 12, which is where the assessment happens.

Status: In progress.

My Thread: Initial Post

295 words · Provenance marking answers a narrow question and is treated as settling the rest

Yes. Provenance marking answers a real question, but a narrow one, and is treated as though it settled the rest.

Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the AI Act has required generative output to be marked machine-readably as synthetic (European Union, 2024), and Anthropic says future Claude models will carry a watermark at launch (Anthropic, 2026).

Golaszewski et al. (2026) put the distinction sharply: despite the word in its name, C2PA is a mechanism for provenance, not authenticity. Origin is not veracity. The same holds for text. Claude’s mark is a version of SynthID-Text (Dathathri et al., 2024), which Anthropic says works poorly on factual passages and is largely absent from code, because exact wording leaves few choices free (Anthropic, 2026).

Such marks are also removable. An open implementation of SynthID-Text lost its watermark in 98.3% of paraphrase runs (Tamim and Khan, 2026), though fragility is the smaller worry.

Even a flawless mark would leave the ethics untouched, because correctness is not by itself a moral property. What carries weight is whether anyone was warranted in putting something forward, and who answers if it turns out wrong. Provenance settles neither.

Consider a synthetic intimate image of a real person. A credential would report only that a model made it, and not who created it, who published it, or that its subject never consented.

So should provenance name the person generating content, and not only the model? It would give the affected person someone to hold responsible. Yet C2PA’s own identity specification concedes that an attacker can lift a valid identity assertion and attach it to another asset, falsely attributing illegal content to an innocent person (Kaye and Dixon, 2025). I am not sure which risk I would rather run. Where would you?

Responses I Wrote on Other Students’ Threads

Unit 10 · Two responses, as the brief requires

Response 1: on where a fairness target comes from

276 words · Peer post narrowed to bias, arguing that generative models intensify inequalities in their training data rather than merely reflecting them

L.K., I think narrowing to bias was the right call. Sun et al. (2024) is careful work too: 15,300 images, 153 occupations, set against 2021 US labour statistics and Google Images. I had to reread their method to be sure, but both are benchmarks. Neither is DALL-E 2’s training distribution, and the authors say as much themselves, describing what they found as potential amplification. Until OpenAI releases the training data, I cannot see how the stronger claim gets tested.

Which leaves your closing question without a purely technical answer. The categories are already a choice, since Sun et al. treat gender as binary. So is the target: match the census and you reproduce occupational segregation, aim for parity and you override the distribution you just measured. Neither looks neutral to me. Gallegos et al. (2024), whom you cite, call fairness value-dependent, and tuning cannot settle a value.

Friedrich et al. (2025) make that harder to put off, since diffusion outputs can be steered to specified demographic proportions without retraining. The capability is there; the principle is not.

Your multi-stage framing points the same way. Mitigation at any stage needs a criterion to be judged against, so the decisive choice sits before data preparation. Gebru et al. (2021) would have dataset composition and collection documented at release, which at least permits the comparison Sun et al. could not run. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (2024) goes further and recommends engaging potentially impacted communities directly.

Article 10(2)(f) and (g) require providers to examine datasets for bias (European Union, 2024), though only for high-risk systems, and a general-purpose image generator is not one by default.

Would you set one target across a model, or let it vary by deployment?

Response 2: on whether upstream or downstream controls carry the weight

276 words · Peer post surveyed bias, copyright and authorship, and memorisation, calling for holistic governance

L.M.F., the range here is useful, and there is one place where two of your issues meet. Moore and Frankel (2024) name them: the problem of the inputs, whether artists’ work can rightfully train a system, and the problem of the outputs, whether anyone can claim credit for the result. Your copyright and memorisation concerns intersect at the training-data boundary, since what enters training can later shape what comes out. The credit question sits apart from both.

Carlini et al. (2023) repay close reading. They extracted over a thousand training examples from state-of-the-art diffusion models, and those images were often duplicated more than a hundred times in the training data. Better deduplication is among their recommendations, matching on image similarity rather than identical URLs. Their finding, though, is that existing techniques, deduplication and differentially private training among them, did not reach an acceptable privacy-utility tradeoff in their experiments.

That does not close off the output end. It does mean downstream safeguards cannot be assumed to remove memorisation, which argues for giving upstream controls more weight inside the governance you propose: screening insufficiently consented material before training, while keeping generation-stage filtering in place. Longpre et al. (2024), treating consent and provenance as broken infrastructure, help explain why downstream measures alone fall short.

Article 53 has required providers of general-purpose AI models to act since August 2025 (European Union, 2024). Its training-content summary, under 53(1)(d), is disclosure. Its copyright policy, under 53(1)(c), must identify and respect rights reservations, which makes it an upstream control.

Your call for holistic governance persuades me. What it leaves open is how each harm maps onto a specific, measurable control. Would you give upstream controls the greater weight, or hold them equal with downstream safeguards?
