The Task

Unit 8 · Formative practical activity · Tutor: Dr Angesh Anupam · Evidences learning outcomes 1 and 2

Unit 8 asks for constituency-based parse trees of three sentences, added to the e-portfolio as the completed artefact:

  1. The government raised interest rates.
  2. The internet gives everyone a voice.
  3. The man saw the dog with the telescope.

The trees are the deliverable, so I have kept the framing brief. The third sentence carries the analytical weight, because it is the one with more than a single correct answer, and that is where an exercise in notation turns into something worth reasoning about.

Constituency, Not Dependency

A parse tree makes the hierarchy inside a sentence explicit. We speak and read in a line, yet the words gather into nested units, and a tree shows those units directly (Zimmerman, 2019). Constituency analysis is one of two common ways to draw that structure. It groups words into phrases, a noun phrase or a verb phrase, and each phrase behaves as one unit that can sit inside a larger one.

The other approach, dependency analysis, links individual words to each other and hangs the whole sentence from the main verb. The difference is not academic for this activity. The set reading demonstrates parsing in the spaCy library, and those diagrams are dependency trees, whereas the brief asks for constituency trees (Zimmerman, 2019). Both describe one grammar from different angles. Everything below keeps to the constituency convention.

A Small Phrase-Structure Grammar

Constituency trees are not drawn by intuition. Each is a derivation from a set of phrase-structure rules, and writing the rules down first makes the trees reproducible rather than merely plausible (Russell and Norvig, 2022, ch. 23). Nine rules generate all three sentences:

S  → NP VP
NP → DT NN
NP → NN NNS
NP → NN
NP → NP PP
VP → VBD NP
VP → VBD NP PP
VP → VBZ NP NP
PP → IN NP

The tags follow the Penn Treebank set (Marcus, Santorini and Marcinkiewicz, 1993). Each tree below appears twice over: as a diagram, and beneath it as the equivalent labelled bracketing. Sentence-final punctuation is left out of the trees, since a full stop carries no constituency and only adds a node.

Sentence 1: The Government Raised Interest Rates

A plain transitive clause with the canonical shape S → NP VP. The subject noun phrase is The government, and the verb phrase is raised interest rates. Within the verb phrase, interest rates is a compound noun phrase (NP → NN NNS) in which interest modifies rates, and the compound as a whole is the direct object of raised.

[S [NP [DT The] [NN government]] [VP [VBD raised] [NP [NN interest] [NNS rates]]]]

Sentence 2: The Internet Gives Everyone a Voice

Here the verb is ditransitive, so the verb phrase branches into two object noun phrases (VP → VBZ NP NP). gives takes an indirect object, everyone, and a direct object, a voice. This is the double-object construction, the pattern behind gives someone something.

[S [NP [DT The] [NN internet]] [VP [VBZ gives] [NP [NN everyone]] [NP [DT a] [NN voice]]]]

One tagging choice is worth flagging. everyone is an indefinite pronoun, yet the Penn Treebank tags it NN rather than as a pronoun, because indefinite pronouns are treated as nouns in that scheme (Marcus, Santorini and Marcinkiewicz, 1993). I have kept NN for consistency with the rest of the notation, and note it here because it is the one label a reader might reasonably expect to differ.

Sentence 3: The Man Saw the Dog With the Telescope

This sentence has two constituency analyses, and nothing in the words themselves decides between them. The difference lies in where the prepositional phrase with the telescope attaches, which is the classic case of prepositional-phrase attachment ambiguity. The highlighted branch in each diagram marks the single structural decision that separates the readings.

Reading A: instrument

The prepositional phrase attaches to the verb phrase (VP → VBD NP PP), so with the telescope modifies the act of seeing. The man used the telescope to see the dog.

[S [NP [DT The] [NN man]] [VP [VBD saw] [NP [DT the] [NN dog]] [PP [IN with] [NP [DT the] [NN telescope]]]]]

Reading B: accompaniment

The prepositional phrase attaches inside the object noun phrase (NP → NP PP), so it modifies the dog. Grammatically this is the same shape as the dog with the collar: with the telescope describes the dog rather than the seeing. What makes this reading strange is not its structure but its content, since a telescope is not something a dog plausibly carries or owns.

[S [NP [DT The] [NN man]] [VP [VBD saw] [NP [NP [DT the] [NN dog]] [PP [IN with] [NP [DT the] [NN telescope]]]]]]

Both trees are grammatical, yet they are not equally plausible. A dog is an unlikely companion for a telescope, so a reader draws on world knowledge and settles almost without noticing on reading A, the instrument. Syntax cannot make that judgement on its own: it offers both parses and ranks neither. This gap, between what the grammar permits and what the world makes likely, is the obstacle a language-using agent has to cross. An information-extraction agent reading the same words must decide whether to record an instrument or an attribute of the dog, and a dialogue agent given an instruction of this shape can act on the wrong object if it guesses. Because structure does not fix the answer, modern parsers attach a probability to each analysis and rank them, learning the likelier attachment from annotated corpora rather than from grammar rules alone (Collins, 2003; Russell and Norvig, 2022, ch. 23). The reading a person discards without noticing is the one a parser has to be taught to rank last.

Conclusion: Where the Tree Runs Out

A constituency tree records how words group into phrases, and that is all it records. Across the three sentences, meaning outruns the structure in three different ways, and each is worth naming, because each is a place a purely syntactic agent would come unstuck.

Sentence 1 parses cleanly, yet the government stands by metonymy for an institution of many people, and whether the government is even the body that sets interest rates is a question of fact that sits entirely outside the tree. Sentence 2 has a single, unambiguous structure, but that structure is identical whether the sentence is read literally or as the metaphor it plainly is: the internet gives nobody a physical voice, and voice here means influence. Sentence 3 is the opposite case, one string with two grammatical trees, where only world knowledge rejects the reading on which the dog, rather than the seeing, is the thing paired with the telescope. Reference and metonymy, figurative meaning, and structural ambiguity: three ways a correct parse still under-determines what a sentence means.

That is the practical limit of the technique. Constituency parsing is a transparent and reproducible foundation, which is exactly why it is worth doing by hand, but a language-using agent has to layer semantics and world knowledge on top of it before the structure becomes an interpretation (Russell and Norvig, 2022, ch. 23).

Reflection

Drawing sentence 3 twice, once for each attachment, did more than meet the brief: two diagrams that differ by a single branch commit an agent to different facts about the world. Sentence 2 taught the same lesson from the other side, since its one tree stays the same whether the giving is read literally or as a metaphor. Taken together they made a small point concrete. A parse can be correct and still leave the meaning open, so I have started to read a parser's output as one hypothesis among several rather than as the analysis. That is the same move from a single point of judgement towards continuous disclosure that the first collaborative discussion argued me into earlier in the module.

This was also a new kind of exercise for me, since I had not built a syntax tree before, and as a non-native English speaker parts of it took an extra step. The bare noun-noun compound in interest rates is one, where French would link the two nouns with a preposition (taux d'intérêt), and the double-object construction in gives everyone a voice is another, since French marks the indirect object with à (donne une voix à chacun) rather than leaving it bare. What helped more than it hindered is that the core phrase structure is close to French. The same S, NP, VP and PP building blocks carry over, and the telescope ambiguity survives translation intact, since l'homme a vu le chien avec le télescope is ambiguous in exactly the same way. The vocabulary looked harder at the outset than the analysis turned out to be.
