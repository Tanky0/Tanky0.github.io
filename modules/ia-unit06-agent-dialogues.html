Creating Agent Dialogues

A KQML and KIF dialogue between a procurement agent and a warehouse agent, with the ontology it depends on and an assessment of where it fails.

Home / Intelligent Agents / Creating Agent Dialogues

The Task

Unit 6 · Formative practical activity · Tutor: Dr Angesh Anupam

The Unit 6 activity asks for an agent dialogue, written in KQML and KIF, between two named agents. Alice is an agent designed to procure stock and Bob is an agent that controls the stock levels for a warehouse. The dialogue should see Alice asking Bob about the available stock of 50 inch televisions, and also querying the number of HDMI slots the televisions have.

The unit learning outcomes attached to it are the ability to develop dialogues using KQML/KIF, and an understanding of the use and deployment of ontologies as a means of knowledge sharing.

I have treated the second outcome as the harder one. Writing eight well-formed messages is a matter of following the syntax in Finin et al. (1994). Deciding what 50 inch and HDMI slot actually denote, and who gets to decide, is where the interesting work sits, so the artefact begins with the ontology rather than with the dialogue.

Why an Agent Communication Language at All

Wooldridge draws the distinction that motivates the whole exercise. If a Java object o2 invokes o1.m1(arg), the decision to execute m1 belongs entirely to o2, and o1 has no say in it (2009, pp. 131-132). An agent cannot be commanded that way, because it is autonomous over both its state and its behaviour. Agents can neither force other agents to act nor write onto another agent's internal state. What they can do is perform communicative actions in an attempt to influence one another.

That framing comes from speech act theory, which begins with Austin's observation that some utterances are themselves actions changing the state of the world (Austin, 1975, cited in University of Essex Online, no date). Searle (1969) then classified illocutionary acts into five kinds, summarised by Wooldridge (2009, pp. 133-134) as representatives, directives, commissives, expressives and declarations. The consequence is visible in one sentence. The door is closed can be a request, an assertion or an enquiry, and only the performative verb distinguishes them, because the propositional content is identical in all three (University of Essex Online, no date).

KQML makes that split structural. The performative comes first and fixes the illocutionary force. Everything else, including the content itself, is an argument (Finin et al., 1994, p. 459). This is what allows Bob to receive (available tv-lx50 wh-01) and know without parsing it whether he is being asked, told or instructed.

The warehouse-stock Ontology

Both agents declare :ontology warehouse-stock on every message. The Unit 5 lecturecast defines an ontology as a formal specification of the set of terms the agents will use, and notes that new terms are normally defined out of existing well understood ones (University of Essex Online, no date). I have followed the same tabular form used for the block world in Unit 5, then given the KIF axioms that make the definitions machine-readable. KIF is based on first-order logic, so the quantifiers and connectives are those covered in Unit 2 (Russell and Norvig, 2022, ch. 8).

Axioms

Class inheritance, which is the mechanism the lecturecast illustrates with the Netflix example, where an instance inherits the attributes of its class and a class those of its superclass:

(forall (?x)
  (=> (Television ?x)
      (Product ?x)))

(forall (?x)
  (=> (Television ?x)
      (and (exists (?s) (= (screen-size ?x) ?s))
           (exists (?n) (= (hdmi-ports ?x) ?n)))))

Availability is defined, not stored, which matters later:

(forall (?x ?w)
  (=> (and (Product ?x) (Warehouse ?w))
      (= (available ?x ?w)
         (- (on-hand ?x ?w) (reserved ?x ?w)))))

And the term the dialogue actually turns on. Alice does not need a primitive for 50 inch television; she builds it from terms Bob already holds, exactly as the lecturecast describes Alice defining Space Agents out of sci-fi, detective story and Blade Runner:

(forall (?x)
  (<=> (FiftyInchTV ?x)
       (and (Television ?x)
            (= (screen-size ?x) (scalar 50 inch)))))

A note on form. KIF provides dedicated defrelation and deffunction constructs for definitions of exactly this kind, and they would be more idiomatic here. I have written the axioms as plain quantified sentences instead, because the set reading for this module establishes only that KIF is based on first-order logic and can be used to express ontologies (University of Essex Online, no date), and the sole KIF the module shows is the ground equality (= (torque m1) (scalar 12 kgf.m)) (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 139). The biconditional carries the same definitional force and stays inside what the module actually supplies.

Two design decisions in that definition are worth naming, because they are the ones that fail in the ontology mismatch section below:

  1. 50 is the nominal marketing size. Whether a supplier's catalogue holds the marketing figure or a measured diagonal is a modelling choice, and this ontology commits to the first without saying so anywhere a reasoner could detect.
  2. hdmi-ports counts sockets and carries no revision. Two sockets of different HDMI generations are indistinguishable under this vocabulary.

The Dialogue

Nine messages, in order. Message flow first, then the messages themselves.

M1. Bob advertises his capability

(advertise
  :sender     bob
  :receiver   alice
  :language   KQML
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :content    (ask-all
                :language KIF
                :ontology warehouse-stock
                :content  (available ?x wh-01)))

advertise is a capability-definition performative, by which a sender claims to be suited to processing a given performative (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). Its content is itself a KQML message, which is why :language on the outer message is KQML and only the inner message declares KIF. This message is what justifies Alice querying Bob at all, in the same way that the stock-ticker advertise justifies a later monitor in Finin et al. (1994, p. 460).

M2. Alice asks whether any 50 inch stock exists

(ask-if
  :sender     alice
  :receiver   bob
  :language   KIF
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :reply-with q1
  :content    (exists (?x)
                (and (FiftyInchTV ?x)
                     (> (available ?x wh-01) 0))))

A directive in Searle's scheme. ask-if asks whether the answer to the content sentence is present in the receiver's virtual knowledge base (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). Note the quantifier: Alice is asking an existential question, not requesting a list, and :reply-with q1 names the query so that any later message can be tied back to it.

M3. Bob answers

(tell
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :language    KIF
  :ontology    warehouse-stock
  :in-reply-to q1
  :content     (exists (?x)
                 (and (FiftyInchTV ?x)
                      (> (available ?x wh-01) 0))))

A representative. With tell, the sender claims that the content sentence is in its own virtual knowledge base (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). The content is repeated verbatim rather than reduced to a token true, which is deliberate: KQML has no boolean reply, so an affirmative answer is the assertion of the queried sentence. Had the answer been negative, Bob would have sent untell, and that carries a weaker claim than most readers expect. untell says the sentence is not in Bob's knowledge base, not that it is false. A warehouse with an unsynchronised feed and a warehouse with genuinely no 50 inch televisions produce the identical message.

M4. Alice asks for models and quantities

(ask-all
  :sender     alice
  :receiver   bob
  :language   KIF
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :reply-with q2
  :content    (and (FiftyInchTV ?x)
                   (= (available ?x wh-01) ?n)))

ask-all requests every answer the receiver holds to the question posed (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). Two free variables, ?x and ?n, so Bob should return the set of pairs that satisfy the conjunction.

M5. Bob replies with the bindings

(reply
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :language    KIF
  :ontology    warehouse-stock
  :in-reply-to q2
  :content     (and (= (available tv-lx50 wh-01) 42)
                    (= (available tv-nb50 wh-01) 7)))

reply simply communicates an expected answer (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). The answer set arrives as one message containing a conjunction of ground KIF sentences. This is legal, but it is not the only reading available. Finin et al. (1994) set out the performative categories without fixing the form an answer set should take, and I read that silence as leaving the choice open between a set of substitutions and a conjunction of instantiated sentences. The inference is mine rather than theirs. If it holds, two conforming implementations can disagree about how to parse M5, which is precisely the interoperability weakness discussed below.

M6. Alice asks about HDMI slots as a stream

(stream-all
  :sender     alice
  :receiver   bob
  :language   KIF
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :reply-with q3
  :content    (and (FiftyInchTV ?x)
                   (= (hdmi-ports ?x) ?n)))

stream-all is the multiple-response version of ask-all. The brief asks about the slots on the televisions, plural, so a stream is the natural fit: each model comes back separately and Alice can begin evaluating the first while the rest are still in flight. This follows the stream-about/tell/tell/eos pattern demonstrated in the Unit 5 lecturecast and in Wooldridge (2009, p. 139, fig. 7.2b).

M7 and M8. Bob streams one answer per model

(tell
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :language    KIF
  :ontology    warehouse-stock
  :in-reply-to q3
  :content     (= (hdmi-ports tv-lx50) 4))

(tell
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :language    KIF
  :ontology    warehouse-stock
  :in-reply-to q3
  :content     (= (hdmi-ports tv-nb50) 3))

M9. End of stream

(eos
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :in-reply-to q3)

eos has no :content and no :language. Wooldridge describes it as "a kind of meta-message" (2009, p. 140), a message about messages. It is worth pausing on that, because it undercuts the tidy story told earlier. eos is not a speech act in Searle's sense at all. It has no propositional content and performs no illocutionary act on Alice's beliefs or intentions. It is flow control that happens to live in the same namespace as tell and ask-if. The performative set is doing two jobs at once, and Wooldridge's complaint that it is "overly large and, it could be argued, rather ad hoc" (2009, p. 140) is at least partly a complaint about this conflation.

Where the Dialogue Breaks: Ontology Mismatch

Suppose Alice's buyer distinguishes between HDMI revisions, which hdmi-ports cannot express, so she has to ask for something the ontology does not carry.

(ask-one
  :sender     alice
  :receiver   bob
  :language   KIF
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :reply-with q4
  :content    (= (hdmi-version tv-lx50) ?v))
(sorry
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :in-reply-to q4)

sorry signals that the sender cannot provide a more informative reply (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). Bob is not refusing, and he is not saying the televisions lack an HDMI version. He is saying that hdmi-version is not a term he can interpret, because warehouse-stock does not define it. The :ontology field named a vocabulary they both claim, and it still failed, because the vocabulary was not rich enough for the question Alice needed to ask.

A worse variant is silent rather than noisy. If Bob's internal catalogue records screen size as a measured diagonal in millimetres, and Alice's FiftyInchTV term expects the nominal marketing figure, then Bob evaluates (= (screen-size ?x) (scalar 50 inch)) against (scalar 1257 mm) and returns nothing. Both agents are behaving correctly. Alice concludes there is no 50 inch stock and goes to another supplier, whilst 42 units sit on Bob's floor. There is no sorry here and no error, only an empty result set that looks exactly like a truthful negative.

This is the semantic heterogeneity problem the Unit 5 lecturecast raises. Payne and Tamma (2014, p. 517) frame it directly: agents are not guaranteed to share a vocabulary, so correspondences have to be selected that give a coherent alignment between their ontologies, and each agent holds only a private, incomplete and typically ambiguous set of those correspondences. Their Correspondence Inclusion Dialogue lets two agents each disclose a subset and still agree an unambiguous alignment, reaching around 95% of the optimum found centrally with complete information whilst each agent discloses on average 16.76% of its own correspondences.

That constraint is not artificial in a procurement setting. Bob's employer has no interest in handing a buyer its full product taxonomy, and Alice's employer has no interest in publishing its buying criteria. The alignment has to be negotiated, partially, in the same message stream as the query. What the dialogue above does instead is assume the problem away by stipulating a shared warehouse-stock ontology in advance, which is the honest limitation of this artefact.

Critical Evaluation of KQML

Strengths the dialogue demonstrates

Force is separated from content. Bob routes on the performative without reading :content. Finin et al. note that all KQML implementations ignore the content portion except to recognise where it begins and ends (1994, p. 459), and their router is deliberately content independent (p. 461). M2 and M3 carry an identical KIF sentence and mean opposite things, which is the door is closed problem solved structurally.

Content language is not fixed. Because :language is declared per message, the same infrastructure carries KIF, Prolog or SQL. M1 carries KQML in its content whilst M2 carries KIF, in one exchange.

Conversations are threadable. :reply-with and :in-reply-to let M7, M8 and M9 be tied to M6 without any assumption of synchronous request and response. This is what makes the asynchronous stream possible at all.

Autonomy is preserved. Bob's sorry above is a legitimate answer. There is no equivalent in a method call, where the only outcomes are a return value or an exception, and neither expresses I understood you and I decline to be more informative.

Weaknesses the dialogue demonstrates

Wooldridge (2009, p. 140) lists five criticisms. Four of them are visible in the nine messages above.

Fluid performative set. Finin et al. concede it themselves, describing the reserved set as "neither a minimal required set nor a closed one" (1994, p. 460). Two implementations can both claim KQML and fail to interoperate.

Undefined transport. Nothing in M1 to M9 says how the bytes reach Bob. Finin et al. report implementations over TCP/IP, email, Linda, HTTP and CORBA (1994, p. 459), so ordering guarantees differ between deployments. For M7, M8 and M9 that matters directly: if the transport does not preserve order, eos can arrive before the second tell, and Alice truncates the answer set.

Informal semantics. The meaning of tell is an English gloss. Nothing lets Alice verify that Bob's tell in M3 reflects a genuine state of his knowledge base rather than an optimistic guess, and this is the point at which I remain least satisfied, as the reflection records.

Overly large and ad hoc set. Forty-one performatives in the version tabulated by Wooldridge (Finin et al., 1993, cited in Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138), mixing illocutionary acts with flow control such as eos, next and discard.

The missing commissives

The fifth criticism deserves its own treatment, because it is the one that stops the dialogue from doing the job the brief implies. Alice is a procurement agent. Having found 42 units, what she actually wants is to hold 20 of them.

(achieve
  :sender     alice
  :receiver   bob
  :language   KIF
  :ontology   warehouse-stock
  :reply-with q5
  :content    (= (reserved tv-lx50 wh-01) 20))

With achieve, a sender asks the receiver to make something true of their environment (Wooldridge, 2009, p. 138). The Unit 5 lecturecast introduces perform for the same job, described as a request to carry out an action of some sort (University of Essex Online, no date). I have used achieve because it appears in the reserved set Wooldridge tabulates whilst perform does not, but either would carry the directive force. It is the right message. The problem is Bob's reply. The only representative available to him is:

(tell
  :sender      bob
  :receiver    alice
  :language    KIF
  :ontology    warehouse-stock
  :in-reply-to q5
  :content     (= (reserved tv-lx50 wh-01) 20))

which asserts that 20 units are reserved, as a fact about the world at the moment of sending. It is not a promise. Nothing in it commits Bob to keeping them reserved for the next thirty seconds, and if a second procurement agent sends the same achieve immediately afterwards, Bob can truthfully tell both. Wooldridge puts it plainly: the language is missing an entire class of performatives, and it is difficult to see how many multiagent scenarios could be implemented without commissives, which appear important if agents are to coordinate their actions (Cohen and Levesque, 1990, cited in Wooldridge, 2009, p. 140).

Searle's five categories included commissives for a reason. A procurement dialogue that cannot express I promise to hold these for you is not a procurement dialogue, it is a catalogue lookup. The available function defined above, which subtracts reserved from on-hand, was written to make this concrete: the ontology has a place to record a commitment, and the language has no act that creates one.

Reflection

My first attempt ran to four messages: ask-if, tell, ask-one, reply. It met the brief. It also proved nothing beyond an ability to copy a syntax, which is not what a portfolio is for. The exercise only became worth doing once I asked what would have to be true before Alice and Bob could understand one another at all, and saw how little of that ever reaches the messages.

Take :ontology warehouse-stock. It reads as a guarantee. It is a claim, and an unverifiable one, because neither agent can inspect the other's vocabulary. The ontology took me longer than the nine messages did. Two commitments buried inside it, a marketing screen size rather than a measured one and a port count blind to revision, are exactly what tends to surface late in my working life, once an integration has already produced numbers nobody trusts.

The parallel with my own work is uncomfortably close. Central laboratory operations exchange data with sponsor systems daily, across organisational boundaries, and what breaks is hardly ever the pipe. It is two parties using one term for two things, undetected until a reconciliation refuses to balance. Payne and Tamma frame the constraint as disclosure: agents may need to align vocabularies neither side will publish. I had filed ontology alignment under schema matching. Read properly, it looks at least as much like a negotiation problem. I gave their remedy more enthusiasm than it has earned, though. CID was proposed in 2014 and tested on benchmark ontologies, not in anyone's warehouse.

What genuinely unsettled me was the commissive gap. Bob's tell in section 7 is true at the moment of sending and worthless as an assurance, and my instinct was to hunt for the performative I had overlooked. None exists. That absence taught me more about what a specification cannot do than any of the messages that worked, and it explains why Wooldridge's criticisms produced FIPA instead of a patched KQML.

Now the limits. This artefact is paper. Nothing here has been implemented, so the awkward parts stayed out of reach: parsing KIF, coping with a stream that arrives out of order, deciding what a router should do with a performative it cannot recognise. My confidence that the KIF is well formed rests on resemblance to published examples, not on having run it. I come from governance rather than development, and the gap between reading a specification closely and building against it is still open. Before Unit 12 I want to implement M2 to M9 as a plain message-passing exercise in Python, where a lost eos or an unhandled sorry fails visibly instead of becoming another paragraph in a reflection.
