Module Overview

Numerical Analysis develops the mathematical and statistical foundations that underpin Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, taught and assessed through applied work in R. The module moves from descriptive statistics and data visualisation, through hypothesis testing and non-parametric alternatives, to correlation, linear and logistic regression, and finally Bayesian reasoning. Each unit adds a technique alongside a harder question about what the evidence can legitimately be asked to show.

Coming into the module with no prior programming experience, the practical work in R (readr, dplyr, haven, ggplot2) represented the steepest learning curve of the programme to date. The module's central contribution to my professional practice has been the shift from treating statistical output as a verdict to treating it as an argument that must be interrogated: which method was used, whether it suited the data, and what the result can actually support.

End of Module Assessment 1: Statistical Analysis Presentation

Brief Description: A 20-minute recorded presentation with transcript analysing the Pima Indian Diabetes Database (768 women, nine variables). The brief required data exploration and descriptive statistics with appropriate visualisations and outlier discussion; diabetes rates across age, BMI and pregnancy categories; and inferential work covering hypothesis testing, a correlation matrix, chi-square association tests, ANOVA across age groups, a multiple linear regression predicting glucose, a logistic regression predicting diabetes outcome evaluated with Hosmer-Lemeshow and classification metrics, and likelihood ratio tests for a BMI×Age interaction. Conclusions and recommendations were required throughout. All analysis and visualisation was performed in R using RStudio.

Approach taken: Zero values in glucose, blood pressure, BMI, skin thickness and insulin were treated as missing rather than valid, reducing the regression sample from 768 to 392 and creating a missingness problem reported openly as a limitation. Where normality could not be assumed, non-parametric tests were run alongside parametric ones (Welch's t-test with Wilcoxon rank-sum; ANOVA with Kruskal-Wallis) rather than relying on a single result. Diagnostic plots were worked through in detail: the scale-location plot showed error variance rising with predicted glucose, meaning confidence intervals become least reliable exactly where diabetic cases concentrate. The logistic model reached 77% accuracy and 87.9% specificity but only 56.8% sensitivity at the 0.50 threshold — 114 missed diagnoses — which drove the recommendation to lower the threshold to 0.30–0.35 and to adopt multiple imputation, cross-validation, and Bayesian logistic regression before any clinical use.

Reflective Piece

The module did not teach me to trust data more. It taught me to trust my own interpretations less until they had been tested through statistical scrutiny.

That realisation built up slowly across twelve increasingly demanding weeks. After more than twenty years in clinical operations and project management, AI governance became my role only recently, and my last real contact with quantitative methods dated back to my economics studies at HEC Lausanne. Coming back to formal statistics was disorienting in a way I had not expected. A lot of what I considered evidence-based reasoning in my professional life had never been checked statistically. I simply assumed it had. Gibbs (1988) writes that experience alone is not enough, and that without reflection, its learning potential is lost; this module kept proving him right.

The Scope of the Learning

The module moved from descriptive statistics and plots in R to hypothesis testing, regression and Bayesian reasoning. Every unit added a technique and a harder question about what the evidence could be asked to show. The artefacts below follow this progression against the module learning outcomes, from cross-tabulations and chi-square through correlation and simple regression to Bayesian conditional probability.

Technical Development and Growing Discomfort

R gave me the most persistent difficulties. I had never programmed before, and the exactness it demands (one wrong variable name and nothing runs) caused real frustration early on. What I slowly understood is that R forces the reasoning into the open. Every assumption has to be written down, which also means it can be questioned. For the Unit 9 cross-tabulations, the brief specified grouping states into regions and banding cases into levels; implementing them in R still left the cut-offs to me, and the table shifted with every boundary I tried. The same applied to the ggplot2 scatter plots on the blood pressure data, where scale and annotation shape what a reader takes away. Unit 8 introduced the Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests; understanding why they fit when normality cannot be assumed felt less like a workaround than learning to pick the correct tool over the comfortable one.

Peer Feedback and Revised Thinking

The formative discussion around Brown (1994) tested these habits in front of others. I had redesigned his Table 2 as a diverging stacked bar chart ranking the reasons discouraging GPs from intrapartum care, and argued that the data pointed towards the willing minority already involved. My peers were not convinced. One colleague, citing Kankaraš and Capecchi (2025), challenged where I had put the no-opinion answers. A neutral midpoint assumes balance, but those responses can just as easily mean uncertainty or reluctance to answer. Another saw a possible selection effect: with cross-sectional data, there is no way to tell whether home deliveries made those GPs willing, or whether they were willing to begin with. They were right on both counts, and seeing the data through their eyes showed me that my chart carried assumptions I never argued. The dot plot I raised in my initial post, separating certainty from direction, still looks the stronger design. Reviewing two peers' charts put me on the other side, questioning their grouping and midpoint choices. Hatton and Smith (1995) rank critical reflection above dialogic reflection because it situates conclusions in a wider context; peer correction moved me in that direction.

Critical Transformation

Nothing changed my thinking more than the work on p-values and confidence intervals. I used to read a p-value as a verdict, significant or not, which is also how most governance documents I encounter treat it. Schober, Bossers and Schwarte (2018) took that reading apart for me: statistical significance and practical importance are different things, and p below 0.05 says nothing about how large an effect is. Confidence intervals turned out to be more useful for decisions, since a range of plausible values keeps uncertainty visible instead of collapsing it. When I reported the 95% interval for my regression slope (0.45 to 0.54), I learnt more from that range than from the p-value next to it.

I cannot separate this from my professional role. If a governance lead is unable to judge the method, they cannot judge whether the conclusion follows from it, and the review becomes ratification rather than oversight. My own governance questions used to stop at controls, ethical risk and regulatory compliance; I rarely asked how statistically sound the evidence underneath was. Informatica (2026) reports that 76% of organisations find their AI governance cannot keep pace with AI usage, a gap attributed to insufficient data literacy that I recognise.

The Bayesian unit pushed this furthest. Van de Schoot et al. (2021) describe Bayesian inference as the principled revision of prior beliefs in the light of new data, with uncertainty treated as part of reasoning rather than a flaw to remove. For AI governance, that framing feels more honest than threshold-based testing, since organisational decisions are rarely final and new evidence should move existing assessments.

New Practice and Professional Development

Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001) would ask what I do differently now. In governance contexts, I no longer let statistical claims pass unexamined. I ask which method was used, whether it suited the data, and what the result can support; the same questions now apply to AI model performance claims inside my organisation.

My professional experience has not lost its value. Reading a situation quickly and framing the right strategic questions still comes naturally to me. What statistics added is a brake, a habit of separating a plausible explanation from a demonstrated one before acting on it. Looking back, the module has not made me trust data more. It has made me slower to trust my own interpretation of it, until the numbers have been given the chance to disagree.
