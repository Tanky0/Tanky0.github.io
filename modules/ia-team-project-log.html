Use Case and Working Method

The brief fixes the domain to academic research. Within it we chose an agent for a clinical and health-sciences research team, to help them screen literature faster for systematic reviews, with retrieval scoped to abstracts and open-access sources on the free PubMed and Semantic Scholar APIs because full text is largely paywalled. That scoping is a challenge for the report, not a gap.

Each member drafts their section independently within two weeks, the sections are combined, and the team then iterates on the merged draft before a final collective review, with content settled first and language left until everything is assembled. A full milestone schedule was judged disproportionate to a 1,000-word report.

Four points of scope came back from the tutor when I put the team’s questions to him. The application domain is fixed for every group, and each group defines its own use case inside it. A full working solution is not expected. Diagrams fall outside the report word limit, and that last answer changed how the graphical designs were planned, since a diagram now carries explanation the prose no longer has room for.

My Contribution

As Group Coordinator I track the schedule, wrote the kick-off minutes, and put the team’s scope and word-count questions to the tutor. From 12 August the role widened, since combining the drafted sections into one document left me editing the report as well as coordinating it.

What I have given so far is review and clinical-domain input across other people’s sections, a role F.B. proposed so the use case would reflect real practice rather than guesswork. To A.N. I sent five requirements carried from clinical operations, among them traceability as a pass-or-fail criterion and an explicit exclusion of clinical decision support, and argued against claiming free-tier capability we cannot build.

Two reviews carried weight. On Section B I checked the cited sources rather than the argument, and found that the Morrison review covers clinical trial matching rather than literature curation, so its 31 studies are not literature pipelines, whilst the error rates quoted from Wang et al. (2026) come from clinical documentation. LEADS (Wang, Z. et al., 2025) fitted better and replaced it. Belief-Desire-Intention was named without doing work in the design, producing an explicit mapping onto the system.

On the assembled report the problem was scale, roughly 2,188 body words against a limit of 1,000. I flagged inconsistent diagram numbering, a section built on a superseded draft, an open API conflated with open access, and a 90-second latency target with nothing behind it.

Combining and editing the report, v12 to v14

Much of the first pass was arithmetic rather than judgement. The merged draft ran to 1,314 words against a limit that costs ten grade points above 1,100, so it would have dropped a band before a marker reached a sentence. The architecture was restated across Sections 1, 3, 5 and 8, and cutting that repetition recovered about 110 words on its own. The rest was formatting the handbook already specifies: Arial 12pt, one-inch margins, italics on source titles and on et al., and eleven em and en dashes taken out of the prose. One en dash stayed inside a published title, where reproducing the source correctly takes priority.

Two substantive problems surfaced in the same pass. The report claimed a free-tier toolchain in Section 1, then relied on a paid fallback two paragraphs later, so the claim was untrue as written; removing the paid option made it true. The three success criteria were aims rather than tests. Retrieval quality was to be confirmed by the reviewer, which makes the acceptance test the same person the system exists to help, so it passes whenever that person is content. It now reads as recall of at least 0.80 against a hand-screened sample of fifty abstracts, set just below the 0.81 LEADS reports so the target is benchmarked rather than invented. Traceability now names what happens when a claim fails to resolve, and fidelity is a twenty-record audit.

v13 and v14 were argument rather than compliance. Section 3 became seven bullets, each giving the decision, the alternative rejected and the reason, which keeps the reasoning that a checklist format tends to flatten into assertion. v14 carried that further after the Unit 4 seminar: why a hybrid agent rather than a purely reactive or deliberative one, why a validated JSON buffer rather than a KQML-style communication language, and why two agents rather than one for every operation. Belief-desire-intention is now described as a conceptual model rather than something formally implemented, which is the more honest account of an LLM system, and two citations moved so each sits beside the claim it supports, Wooldridge (2009) with the hybrid architecture.

Three things were argued out and deliberately left out. The MCP institutional database connector sat outside the fixed domain and contradicted our own no-patient-data assumption. PRISMA-S was not added, since one PRISMA reference is proportionate at this length. The publisher litigation angle went because no citable source supported it.

The division of work shifted as the report came together, by agreement rather than by drift. Alignment on scope and structure came early, which freed the others to concentrate on their own components and on the module activities running alongside, and the writing adjustments stayed with me. A change log goes out with every version, each edit carrying its reason, so any decision remains open to challenge from whoever wants to take it up.
