Scope of This Log

Covers Units 1 and 2 · Updated as the project proceeds · Evidences learning outcomes 1 to 4

The Development Team Project asks a team of four, positioned as software consultants, to produce a 1,000-word design proposal for an LLM-powered planning agent. The domain is fixed by the brief: academic research and information gathering. The team submits one report, and each member submits an individual peer assessment separately. Both are due by the end of Unit 6. The assignment carries 20% of the module mark.

This log records what the team decided, when, and on what reasoning. It stops at the end of Unit 2. Later entries will be added as the project runs.

Team and Contract

Group B has four members: Ali, Fedi, Abdullah and myself. The signed team contract was returned by Friday 31 July, the date the tutor confirmed in the Unit 1 seminar on 29 July.

Several contract terms have already shaped how the work runs, so they are worth recording rather than filing:

  • An internal deadline of 4 September, three days ahead of the official 7 September cut-off, so that a late section does not become a late submission.
  • A fixed word budget per section: 250 for A, 300 for B, 250 for C, 200 for D. Anyone over cuts their own section back before handing it on, rather than leaving the merge to absorb it.
  • One master document with a named owner. We draft separately and the owner merges, so changes to the merged version go through one person.
  • Domain and architecture locked on 8 August and not reopened without unanimous agreement.
  • Decisions by agreement, and by majority where agreement fails. No project lead was appointed; the coordinator tracks the schedule but decisions belong to the group.
  • AI tools permitted for support, with everything submitted required to be our own, checked against sources, and in line with university guidance on AI in summative assessments.

Four team roles were defined alongside the four report sections: coordinator and deadline tracker, meeting organiser and minutes taker, document owner and submitter, and quality and referencing lead. Roles were not rotated, on the reasoning that a six-week project benefits more from continuity than from breadth of exposure.

Why this domain

The concept is an LLM-powered research assistant that helps researchers search, retrieve and summarise scientific literature from publicly available sources. It is not speculative: comparable tools already operate, including OpenEvidence, automated clinical trial screening systems, and general deep research tools.

The feasibility check mattered more than the concept. PubMed and Semantic Scholar both expose free APIs, which fits the free-tier constraint the brief imposes. Full-text journal content is largely paywalled, so retrieval is scoped to abstracts and open-access sources. That limitation is not a weakness to hide; it is one of the anticipated challenges Section C has to address.

The domain also carries a live ethics angle. Major publishers are currently litigating against AI companies over training on academic content, which bears directly on how our own agent should source material. Intellectual property, attribution, reliability of AI-generated summaries and responsible use of LLMs in research all sit naturally in this domain rather than being bolted on.

My Contribution

My workstream is Sections C and D with Abdullah: anticipated challenges and mitigations, the graphical designs, and the final document review.

Setting the frame, 29 July

I posted the opening proposal before the kick-off. It read the brief, the criteria grid and the unit materials, and turned them into a concrete starting position: two candidate domains, a four-way split with word budgets, four roles, and a schedule with an internal deadline.

Two judgements in it have held up. The first is that 1,000 words for four people is the binding constraint, so bulleted lists are the only realistic format and depth has to come from precise use of module vocabulary rather than volume. The second is that we should commit early to justifying the architecture against the Unit 3 and 4 taxonomy, use BDI terminology precisely, and specify inter-agent communication with an agent communication language and a shared ontology from Units 5 and 6. Naming those costs very few words and signals module-specific knowledge immediately, which matters at this length.

I also said plainly where I am weak. My background is strategy, governance, project management and writing rather than hands-on implementation, so I asked not to own the technical architecture and suggested Section B go to whoever was confident there. It did.

Requirements analysis for the health-sciences domain

Once the domain settled I produced a structured requirements analysis for AI literature discovery, organised into eleven categories: scientific, workflow, explainability, legal, regulatory, research integrity, ethical, reproducibility, transparency, security and integration, plus a set of desired agentic capabilities.

It is deliberately larger than the report can use. Its purpose is to give Sections A and C a menu to select from with the reasoning already attached, rather than to be pasted in. Several strands in it come straight from clinical operations practice: traceability as an acceptance criterion rather than a feature, version status as a field so that retracted or superseded papers are detectable, and reproducible search logging so that a query can be re-run for a systematic review or an audit.

Input to Section A, 3 August

Forum post to the Section A owner · 3 August 2026

Five requirements carried across from healthcare work, each argued on its own merits rather than on provenance: traceability as a pass-or-fail acceptance criterion; version status as a field; reproducible search; a declared language scope stated as an assumption rather than a capability; and an explicit exclusion of clinical decision support.

The post flagged its own uncertainties. I had not verified that the free Crossref API exposes retraction metadata in the form we would need, and said so rather than letting it pass into the report unchecked. I also recorded that I had reversed my own earlier position on translation, moving it from requirements to exclusions once I noticed it contradicted the paragraph below it.

The substantive argument was against listing capabilities we cannot build on a free tier. Comprehensive multi-publisher coverage, citation graph exploration and continuous monitoring are all genuine researcher needs, but claiming them weakens the feasibility case the rest of the report rests on. Naming two or three as acknowledged exclusions is more honest, and probably marks better.

The post was framed as options rather than instructions, and said so in its opening line. Section A belongs to its owner; my role was to surface considerations he might not otherwise have, and to be explicit about which of them I was confident in.

Tutor clarifications obtained

  • The application domain is fixed. Within it, each group defines its own use case, target users, organisational context and agent design.
  • A full working solution is not required for this assessment. The proposal must be detailed and realistic enough that it could be implemented with the technologies specified.
  • Diagrams, figures, tables and UML models do not count towards the 1,000 words. Explanatory text in the main body does.

The third point changed how Section D is planned. With diagrams outside the count, the graphical designs can carry more of the explanatory load than the 200-word budget alone would allow.
